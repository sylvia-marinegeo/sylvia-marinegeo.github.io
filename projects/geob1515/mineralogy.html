GeoB1515-1 · Mineralogical Analysis

Carbonate Mineralogy

An investigation of carbonate mineralogy in western equatorial Atlantic sediments, integrating quantitative X-ray diffraction, sedimentological observations, and multiproxy analysis to explore carbonate origins, depositional processes, and paleoceanographic variability.

Sylvia Hwang · B.Sc. Marine Geosciences · Universität Bremen

Carbonate Stratigraphy

Selected XRD measurements illustrate how carbonate mineral abundances vary with sediment depth.

Carbonate Mineral Distribution

The analysis focuses on calcite, aragonite, and other carbonate phases. Depth profiles are used to investigate changes in mineral composition and identify intervals for further examination.

Key Mineralogical Observations

X-ray diffraction measurements reveal variations in carbonate mineral assemblages throughout the GeoB1515-1 sediment sequence. Three features are particularly notable.

01

Near-Surface Aragonite Enrichment

Aragonite is relatively abundant within the upper 25 cm of the sediment core, with substantially lower reported abundances below this interval.

Geological interpretation: Pteropod remains documented in the original expedition report provide a plausible biogenic source for the near-surface aragonite. Preservation and dissolution may also influence its distribution.

02

Deeper Aragonite Occurrence

A localized interval of elevated aragonite abundance occurs at approximately 418–428 cm.

Geological interpretation: The original expedition report identifies a turbidite near 428 cm, associated with grain-size sorting and pteropod fragments. Sediment redeposition may explain the localized aragonite enrichment.

03

Unusual Carbonate Phase at 203 cm

An anomalous abundance is reported in the combined siderite/magnesite category at 203 cm. This category is absent or nearly absent in most other samples.

Further investigation: Examination of the original diffraction pattern is required to evaluate the mineral identification.

Biogenic Origins of the Carbonate Assemblage

One of the questions I wanted to investigate was where the carbonate minerals in GeoB1515-1 originated. The XRD measurements show that low-Mg calcite is the dominant carbonate phase throughout much of the core, but mineral identification alone cannot establish the biological sources of that calcite.

To investigate this further, I returned to the original METEOR M16/2 expedition report (Wefer et al., 1991). The report includes microscopic observations of sediment composition, providing an independent line of evidence alongside the XRD measurements.

Calcareous Nannofossils and Foraminifera

Figure 21 of the expedition report documents the distribution of calcareous nannofossils, foraminifera, and clay minerals throughout GeoB1515-1. These observations support a substantial biogenic contribution to the carbonate assemblage.

Calcareous nannofossils and many planktonic foraminifera produce calcitic skeletal material. Their occurrence is therefore consistent with the predominance of low-Mg calcite identified by XRD.

The two analytical approaches provide complementary information. XRD identifies crystalline mineral phases and estimates their relative abundances, while microscopic observations help identify the sedimentary components and organisms contributing those minerals.

Near-Surface Aragonite

Another interesting observation is the presence of aragonite within the upper 25 cm of the core.

The expedition report documents pteropod remains within the upper 15 cm. Because pteropod shells are typically composed of aragonite, their presence provides a plausible biological source for at least some of the near-surface aragonite detected by XRD.

However, the presence of aragonitic organisms does not necessarily explain the entire aragonite distribution. Preservation, dissolution, and sediment transport may also influence the abundance of aragonite in the sediment record.

The microscopic observations reported during the expedition and the quantitative XRD measurements represent different analytical approaches. Microscopic estimates of sediment components should not be interpreted as directly equivalent to XRD mineral percentages.

Bulk Sediment Composition

The relative abundances of carbonate minerals and major silicates vary throughout the sediment sequence. Comparing these mineral groups provides an overview of changes in bulk sediment composition.

Interpretation

Carbonate and major silicate abundances exhibit a strong negative correlation (r = −0.932) across the analyzed samples. Carbonate-rich intervals generally correspond to lower proportions of major silicate minerals.

Because mineral abundances are expressed as percentages of the same bulk sediment, this relationship is partly influenced by the constant-sum constraint of compositional data. The observed anticorrelation should therefore not be interpreted as direct evidence of opposing sediment fluxes.

Multiproxy Relationships

After examining the carbonate mineral assemblage, I wanted to investigate whether changes in carbonate abundance were reflected in other sediment properties. To explore this, I compared the XRD measurements with sediment lightness and magnetic susceptibility along the core.

The comparison revealed two moderate relationships. Carbonate abundance is positively correlated with sediment lightness (r = +0.40) and negatively correlated with magnetic susceptibility (r = −0.42).

These relationships suggest that changes in carbonate abundance are associated with broader variations in sediment composition. Carbonate-rich intervals tend to be lighter in color and exhibit lower magnetic susceptibility, although neither relationship is strong enough to predict carbonate abundance reliably.

Interpreting the Relationships

The positive relationship between carbonate abundance and sediment lightness is consistent with the presence of light-colored biogenic carbonate material. However, sediment color can also be influenced by clay minerals, iron-bearing phases, and other components of the sediment.

The negative relationship with magnetic susceptibility may reflect changes in the relative contributions of carbonate and magnetically susceptible minerals. Magnetic susceptibility is influenced by magnetic mineral concentration, composition, and grain size, so it cannot be interpreted as a direct measure of terrigenous sediment supply.

Carbonate Preservation

Another possible influence on carbonate abundance is preservation after deposition. The original METEOR M16/2 expedition report includes observations of carbonate preservation in GeoB1515-1 (Wefer et al., 1991, Figure 38).

These observations provide an additional perspective on the carbonate record. Variations in carbonate abundance may reflect differences in biological carbonate supply, dilution by noncarbonate sediment, or dissolution and preservation on the seafloor.

However, the expedition's qualitative preservation observations should not be treated as quantitative measurements of carbonate dissolution. The available evidence does not yet allow the relative contributions of these processes to be determined.

Carbonate Deposition Across the Last Deglaciation

The multiproxy comparison revealed relationships between carbonate abundance, sediment lightness, and magnetic susceptibility. I wanted to investigate whether these relationships could also be placed within a climatic context.

For this part of the investigation, I used the published chronology for the upper 95 cm of GeoB1515-1, which extends to approximately 30.4 ka. This allowed me to examine changes in sediment composition across the Last Glacial Maximum and the subsequent transition toward the Holocene.

Changes in Carbonate Abundance

Carbonate abundance was generally lower during the Last Glacial Maximum. As the climate transitioned toward the Holocene, carbonate abundance increased substantially.

This increase was accompanied by a general increase in sediment lightness and a decrease in magnetic susceptibility. Together, these observations suggest a change in the relative contributions of carbonate and noncarbonate material to the sediment.

Possible Depositional Controls

One possible explanation for the deglacial carbonate enrichment is a change in the supply of noncarbonate sediment. During periods of lower sea level, continental shelves were more exposed, potentially influencing sediment transport and delivery to the deep ocean.

As sea level rose during deglaciation, changes in sediment routing and terrigenous sediment supply may have reduced the dilution of biogenic carbonate within the sediment. This could produce higher carbonate percentages even without a corresponding increase in carbonate production.

However, changes in biological productivity and carbonate preservation may also have contributed to the observed pattern. The available mineralogical and physical-property measurements do not allow these mechanisms to be distinguished conclusively.

Carbonate Concentration Versus Accumulation

An important limitation of this interpretation is that the XRD measurements represent relative mineral abundances rather than carbonate accumulation rates.

An increase in carbonate percentage does not necessarily indicate an increase in carbonate productivity or deposition. For example, a reduction in the supply of noncarbonate sediment could increase the relative proportion of carbonate even if the amount of carbonate reaching the seafloor remained unchanged.

Distinguishing between these possibilities would require additional constraints, such as sediment mass accumulation rates and independent indicators of carbonate production or preservation.

This interpretation is restricted to the upper 95 cm, where published age assignments are available. The exploratory age-depth models developed for the deeper sediment sequence are not used to assign ages to the carbonate variations discussed here.

Aragonite Enrichment and Sediment Redeposition

While examining the carbonate mineral profiles, I noticed a localized increase in aragonite abundance between approximately 418 and 428 cm. This interval stood out because aragonite is otherwise largely absent from much of the deeper sediment sequence.

Initially, I considered whether this enrichment might reflect changes in carbonate preservation or biological carbonate production. However, the original METEOR M16/2 expedition report provided another possible explanation.

Evidence from the Original Expedition

Wefer et al. (1991) identified a turbidite at approximately 428 cm in GeoB1515-1, based on grain-size sorting and the presence of pteropod fragments.

This observation is particularly relevant because pteropod shells are typically composed of aragonite. The occurrence of aragonite in the XRD measurements near the reported turbidite therefore raises the possibility that the mineralogical enrichment reflects transported shell material rather than changes in local carbonate production.

A Possible Transport Mechanism

Turbidity currents transport sediment downslope through sediment-gravity flows. These events can introduce material from shallower environments into deeper depositional settings, potentially producing mineralogical compositions that differ from the surrounding background sediment.

If the aragonite enrichment is associated with the reported turbidite, the event may have transported and redeposited aragonitic shell fragments within the sediment sequence.

This interpretation is consistent with both the mineralogical measurements and the original sedimentological observations, although their correspondence does not independently establish the source of the transported material.

Implications for Interpreting the Core

This finding illustrates why localized mineralogical anomalies should not automatically be interpreted as changes in regional oceanographic conditions. Individual depositional events can alter sediment composition independently of longer-term changes in biological productivity or climate.

The aragonite enrichment at 418–428 cm may therefore represent a localized sedimentary event rather than a sustained change in carbonate production or preservation.

The proposed association between aragonite enrichment and sediment redeposition is based on the correspondence between XRD measurements and published sedimentological observations. Additional grain-size, microscopic, or sedimentological analyses would be needed to confirm the origin of the aragonitic material.

Synthesis and Limitations

Bringing together the XRD measurements, sediment physical properties, and observations from the original expedition has helped me develop a more complete interpretation of the carbonate mineralogy of GeoB1515-1.

What the Evidence Suggests

The predominance of low-Mg calcite, together with the documented occurrence of calcareous nannofossils and foraminifera, supports a substantial biogenic contribution to the carbonate assemblage. Pteropod remains also provide a plausible source for some of the aragonite identified by XRD.

Carbonate abundance is moderately associated with sediment lightness and magnetic susceptibility. These relationships suggest that mineralogical variability is connected to broader changes in sediment composition, although they do not establish the processes responsible.

Within the chronologically constrained upper core, carbonate enrichment during the last deglaciation coincides with generally increasing sediment lightness and decreasing magnetic susceptibility. Changes in noncarbonate sediment supply may have contributed to this pattern, alongside possible changes in carbonate production and preservation.

At greater depth, the localized aragonite enrichment near 418–428 cm corresponds closely with a turbidite reported during the original expedition. This suggests that sediment redeposition may explain at least some mineralogical variations independently of longer-term climatic processes.

Remaining Uncertainties

Several limitations remain important when interpreting these results.

First, quantitative XRD measurements describe relative mineral abundances. Without sediment accumulation rates and dry bulk density, changes in carbonate concentration cannot be translated directly into carbonate mass accumulation rates.

Second, correlations between carbonate abundance and sediment physical properties do not establish causation. Changes in sediment supply, carbonate preservation, and biological production may contribute simultaneously to the observed patterns.

Finally, the published chronology provides a temporal framework for the upper 95 cm, but reliable age assignments remain unavailable for much of the deeper sediment sequence. Interpretations of deeper mineralogical features therefore remain depth-based rather than tied to specific climatic events.

What I Took Away from This Investigation

One of the most useful aspects of this project was discovering how much additional information could be obtained by comparing independent analytical methods.

The XRD measurements identified changes in mineral composition, but the original expedition observations and sediment physical properties provided the geological context needed to investigate their possible origins.

The investigation also demonstrated the importance of distinguishing observations from interpretations. Some patterns have plausible explanations, while others remain unresolved. Recognizing those limitations is an important part of developing a defensible geological interpretation.
